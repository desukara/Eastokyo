The End of the Easy Teacher

How Japan's English schools spent twenty years hiring people who wanted to come to Japan—and are only now discovering that this was never the same thing as hiring teachers.

By James SimmonsEastokyo MagazineTokyo · 2026
Editor's note: The names of the sources quoted in this piece are pseudonyms. Each spoke on condition of anonymity, citing concerns about employment and immigration status.

A Decision

Yuki Nakamura, who has owned an English school in Tokyo for more than two decades, had already made up his mind before he finished reading the CV. The candidate was strong on paper—a university degree, teaching experience in two countries, glowing references, the kind of application that would have got an automatic yes ten years ago. Nakamura chose someone else instead: a weaker candidate by the usual measures, but one who already lived in Yokohama, already had a bank account, a lease, a tax history and a visa that did not need to be conjured out of an overseas job offer.

“There may be more applicants than ever before in my career,” Nakamura said. “And yet I feel less confident in my decisions than I did ten years ago.” He was not describing a labour shortage. Applications, he said, had never been more plentiful.

The Old Arrangement

For most of the last three decades, Japan's English education industry ran on an assumption nobody examined too closely: that the number of foreigners who wanted to come to Japan and the number who could reliably be employed as teachers there were, for practical purposes, the same number.

A school needed a foreign face in front of a classroom. An applicant wanted a plane ticket and residence card. Sponsorship became a transaction rather than a decision. The legal and operational plumbing still exists. What has changed is that someone is finally checking it.

The Recruiter

Kenji Sato has spent eight years placing foreign teachers in Japanese schools. What he has watched happen is schools slowly discovering that an abundance of interest is not the same thing as an abundance of options. “Schools think they have the upper hand because they have lots of applicants,” he said. “They don't have the upper hand with good candidates.”

Formalisation, Not Crackdown

Hiroshi Tanaka, an immigration specialist nearing the end of a decades-long career, did not like the word crackdown. He preferred formalisation. Documentation must be accurate. Contracts must reflect actual duties. Insurance obligations must be met. These were not new demands, he said, but things that were always technically true and simply were not always checked.

“For an organisation with a strong system already, the change is small,” he said. “For an organisation that depended on flexibility, the change is very large.”

The Ones Who Leave

David Carter arrived in Japan in 2008 and stayed nearly twenty years—long enough to build a version of a life that, by his account, no longer quite exists for people arriving today. Eventually teachers start asking the questions that end careers. Can I buy a place here? Can I raise children here? Can I retire without disaster? “For a lot of us, the answer's just no.”

The Vehicle

Sarah Williams arrived in Japan less than three years ago, drawn by a plan she had first made as a teenager. Asked whether she came because she wanted to teach, or taught because she wanted to come, she laughed and then stopped laughing. “Honestly? I taught because I wanted to come to Japan.”

For years, English schools built hiring pipelines on the premise that wanting to live in Japan and wanting to teach English were functionally the same motivation. They are not the same door.

The Odds

None of the five sources described a country turning against foreign workers. What came through was something closer to an audit arriving twenty years late—an industry that had always technically required competence, stability and compliance, only now beginning to check for them.

What has disappeared is not the applicants or the demand, but the industry's ability to treat popularity and reliability as the same currency. Japan does not have a shortage of foreigners who want to come. It has a shortage of foreign teachers who are simultaneously qualified, compliant, financially durable and willing to stay long enough to justify the cost of sponsoring them.
