The Hire You're Actually Making

A school posting a vacancy thinks it is filling a role. What it is actually doing is placing a long-term bet on continuity—and that bet is decided long before the first lesson is taught.

By James SimmonsEastokyo Education ReviewTokyo · 2026

It is a strange thing about hiring in this industry: the decision that most determines a school's stability over the next two years is made in a single afternoon, often under time pressure, using an interview format that was never designed to answer the question that actually matters. Schools know this in the abstract. Almost none of them act on it, because acting on it requires slowing down at precisely the moment everything in the business is telling them to speed up.

The System View

A CV answers one question: can this person teach English competently? For most candidates who make it to interview stage, the honest answer is yes. A trial lesson, a few pointed questions about classroom management and a look at prior experience can produce a reasonably confident answer within an hour.

But the CV and interview answer almost nothing about the question that determines outcomes over a two-year horizon: will this person still be in the classroom, functioning well, when visa renewals, cultural adjustment, a difficult term or a homesick winter arrive? These are not edge cases. They are ordinary conditions of the job for a foreign teacher living and working in Japan for the first time.

Schools that hire on the first question and discover the second too late are the ones cycling through turnover every few terms—not because they are hiring badly in an obvious sense, but because they are optimising for the wrong variable. The hire is not a staffing transaction. It is a forecast.

The View From the Interview Room

Sit through enough hiring rounds at an eikaiwa and a pattern emerges. The candidates who struggle are not usually the ones with weak English or thin teaching credentials. They are often the people who interview beautifully: articulate, warm, capable in front of a classroom—and then discover that they underestimated what it means to be the only foreign member of staff in a small Japanese workplace.

This is a mismatch distinct from a simple skills gap. A teacher who struggles with lesson planning can usually be coached. A teacher quietly worn down by unfamiliar hierarchy, scheduling norms and workplace communication may not present as a performance problem until the moment they resign. From the school's perspective, the departure feels sudden. From the teacher's perspective, it rarely is.

Adaptability and charisma are not the same trait, and interviews are much better at surfacing the second. The schools that hire well ask less about polished self-presentation and more about how a candidate has handled disorientation before: a move abroad, a difficult workplace, or a period without a clear playbook.

The Operational Discipline

Reframed as a continuity decision rather than a recruitment task, hiring foreign teachers benefits from the same rigour applied to any high-consequence, hard-to-reverse choice.

Screen for adaptability as a distinct criterion, not a soft afterthought. Behavioural questions are more useful than asking whether somebody considers themselves flexible. “Tell me about a time you had to function in a system you did not fully understand” reveals more than a rehearsed claim of adaptability ever will.

Separate the hiring clock from the operational clock. Rushed decisions cluster around staffing emergencies—exactly when a school is least able to evaluate long-term fit. Interim cover, a redistributed timetable or another bridge arrangement can remove urgency from the permanent decision.

Plan for disruption, not only success. Even a strong candidate can face a visa delay, family emergency or changed circumstance. A careful hiring process and a sound contingency plan are not substitutes. A school needs both.

The Business Case

The economics favour patience. A rushed hire that fails costs the original recruitment spend, onboarding time and then a second recruitment cycle. It also creates a second disruption for the same pupils and parents, at exactly the moment the school most needs to demonstrate control.

Schools that can wait for the right candidate because interim cover is in place are effectively buying insurance against the much larger cost of hiring twice. A slightly longer search is visible and immediate. A bad hire is larger, delayed and often disguised as a resignation or difficult performance conversation.

Temporary bridge support therefore stops looking like an admission of failure and starts looking like what it is: a deliberate tool that gives a school time to hire well.

The Human Stakes

Behind every hiring decision is a classroom of pupils who may spend years with whoever is chosen. From the school's side, recruitment can become postings, CVs, interviews and reference checks. From a child's side, the hire is the person who greets them every week, remembers the piano recital and becomes one of the fixed points of their routine.

A poor hire, or one made in haste, risks becoming another disruption in a sequence the pupils had no part in creating. Children adapt to a new teacher. They adapt less easily to teachers arriving and leaving in quick succession, each departure quietly teaching them not to become too attached.

Getting the hire right is not, in the end, a CV exercise. It is a promise about who will keep showing up—and it deserves the time required to keep that promise well.

Reporting drawn from patterns observed across English-language schools in Japan navigating staffing continuity.
