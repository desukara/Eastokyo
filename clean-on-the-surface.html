Issue No. 01 · August 2026 · Cover Story

Clean on
the Surface

Tokyo looks ready for inspection, undoubtedly. But what does that spotless surface ask us not to notice?

Words and photography by James Simmons · Tokyo, Japan

A pile of trash sitting outside a train station in Tokyo gets more special treatment than a lot of the people here. Trash disposal is taken so seriously that foreigners are often criticized for not understanding the system. And without written instructions, it really is too complicated. Every part of the disposal process follows an intricate plan that leaves no room for guesswork. Anyone who’s been to Tokyo will tell you some version of the same thing: it’s sooo clean. That reputation is well earned. Nobody here is saying Tokyo is lowkey untidy.

What this article is asking is what it takes to maintain those standards—and what happens to the people struggling under the pressure to meet them? Much of Japanese public life depends on people controlling their actions and considering others. That’s one reason Tokyo is so livable and people here are so over-the-top polite. But the same expectations can make it harder to admit when something is wrong. People still smile and bow professionally when they’re almost too exhausted from overwork to stand. Rule-following is not the problem. The problem begins when protecting the appearance of order matters more than protecting the people holding up the system.

01 · Ready for Inspection

The city everyone photographs.

Start with the owl. A hedge is carefully clipped into the shape of a happy owl with a cartoon face. You almost can’t help but stop and take a picture. So what’s the purpose of having this gigantic hedge shaped into a lovable owl when an ordinary bush would have been practical? Tokyo almost never settles for boring when it could be more adorable. Greenery becomes gigantic cartoon owls, and construction barriers turn into smiling cartoon mascots. It’s all about presentation.

To be real with you, Tokyo treats most things like a product that has to be presented to the very highest standards. That’s why Tokyo is world-famous for having some of the best customer service anywhere on Earth, and visitors come here fully expecting the red carpet to be rolled out. For the most part, that’s exactly what they encounter. The thing is, they’re not being fake. None of it is fake. It’s the finished product.

Outside Ikebukuro Station, swarms of people wait patiently at the light without even thinking about jaywalking. That’s unheard of here. Then, as soon as the walk sign turns green, crowds move through the crosswalk in what one could only describe as a symphony of extremely organized chaos. There are thousands of adjustments and nonverbal cues. That’s one of the reasons this megacity works like a well-oiled machine. People are constantly regulating their behavior on behalf of the collective group. The group itself is a unit. One band, one beat.

Honestly, this city would not be as functional without the rules. This article does not claim the rules should all go out the window. Tokyo would be in total chaos trying to manage all these people in such a condensed area. But when someone would rather suffer in a dark corner for weeks or months than bother someone with a legitimate life emergency, it’s time to reevaluate. People stay quiet not because they don’t have something real to say, but because making their situation visible might bother someone or change the rhythm of the group. There has to be a point when it’s okay to press pause or go to commercial break. In other words, order becomes dangerous when it is more important than the people struggling to keep up with it.

02 · Perfect Service

Looking fine is part of the job.

The robot never has a bad night’s sleep nor wakes up on the wrong side of the bed. You don’t have to worry if he’s going to get pissed or need a little time to cool off. He’s not carrying childhood trauma or wondering if his colleagues really have his back. Hell na. The robot always has it together, ready with a great answer and not even thinking about how many friends he has on FB. That, dear reader, is the whole fantasy of frictionless service.

Japanese customer service is world-famous for a reason. Everything from the greetings to the beautiful packaging is genuine craftsmanship at the highest level. But Japanese people are not robots, and some of that effort may be hiding pain. They are only human, with the daily task of performing without letting anyone see the tiredness, bad nerves, dilemmas, or constant pressures. The nearly impossible job of measuring up to the ultra-high standards here always has to seem effortless, especially when it’s not. A perfect performance with a smile tells us a lot about the standards. But it doesn’t tell us that much about the real person behind the mask.

03 · Backstage Tokyo

Convenience has a loading dock.

Every flawless storefront is just the face of a messy process behind the scenes. Whether it’s a restaurant, hotel, 7-Eleven, or grocery store, they all depend on deliveries making it on time. Then there’s the other stuff, like storage, refrigeration, trash, and big trucks trying to navigate narrow side streets full of pedestrians. The paying customers only see the clean entrances, careful displays, and organized products. That’s because the dirty part of the process stays out of public view. But someone has to absorb the everyday hiccups. Who’s getting inconvenienced? The better the public side looks, the more pressure they’re dealing with to make it all work out.

Then we have the smiling guy next to the crack. Doesn’t he look so happy to oblige? That smile never changes, but the wall beside him is starting to split like the Red Sea. This photograph isn’t saying that Tokyo itself is cracking up. But it does show something smaller and more familiar: a reassuring message that everything’s all good can be right beside obvious signs of major strain.

A company can give the impression that they’re one big happy family while the people working there can’t even speak their minds. An institution can ritually apologize for inconveniences without acknowledging what caused the problem. And rejection can show up as a neutral rule instead of an honest explanation. See, politeness can prevent conflict and be a form of genuine care for others. It can also become a way of avoiding a difficult truth.

In fiscal year 2025, Japan’s labor ministry approved 1,310 workers’ compensation cases for work-related brain or heart diseases and mental disorders. Out of those, 145 involved death, suicide, or attempted suicide. Don’t take that lightly. These were officially verified cases, and they are not a panoramic report of all workplace illnesses and distress. Clean sidewalks and manicured bushes don’t overwork people. Just like saying gomenasai and waiting in line for your turn don’t cause a mental breakdown or suicide attempt. But a picture’s worth a thousand words, and together they raise the same question as those statistics: What happens when keeping the surface clean becomes more important than the person who’s cleaning the surface?

04 · The Work Underneath

Perfection is never effortless.

Let’s get back to the trash beneath the blue net. It didn’t just get up and walk away. Somebody drove there, picked it up with their hands, and brought it where it needed to go. That’s where civic virtue gets up close and personal. There’s weight involved, traffic, summer heat, and filthy bags with who knows what in them. All this is happening right beside ordinary street life without bothering anyone or making a big scene. People keep walking, and shops stay open while the junk keeps vanishing. Cleanliness at Tokyo street level is not an attitude. It’s a lot of sweat and disgusting shit that somebody had to clean up.

The second photograph changes the vibe. This man isn’t just some guy in a uniform cleaning up the streets of Tokyo. He’s one of the heroes that most people would never think to thank. There he is, standing proudly beside his company truck with his head held high. We see you, man. Thank you.

He agreed to be photographed and asked that the company name be in the photographs. We were able to talk with this nice man, Edgar from the Philippines, who is working very hard to maintain Tokyo’s standards. We saw him working hard in the sun, in temperatures over 101 degrees Fahrenheit, and not slowing down for a break. The photograph asks us to see the real person behind the scenes. He’s one of very many who keep everything spotless so Tokyo can maintain its world-famous reputation for cleanliness and efficiency.

These unseen heroes are skilled, resourceful, and a source of pride in Tokyo. The point is not that every worker behind the scenes is secretly miserable. That’s not what we’re saying. We’re saying that the streets are well maintained because someone is taking care of them.

05 · When the Surface Smudges

Nothing disappears just because it moves out of sight.

Behind the spotless streets that tourists love to talk about is an alley. There’s trash on the ground and plastic crates against the walls. That’s just the other side of commercial life, and it shouldn’t come as a shock. That’s the point. This is where the rules of perfect presentation don’t count. The main streets and main tourist areas get all the attention because that’s what everyone sees. The back alleys have to handle whatever doesn’t look nice out front: trash, beer kegs, filthy kitchen rags, and broken dishes. A great presentation usually depends on somewhere off to the side that won’t be in public view.

That same logic can also apply to people who are pushed off to the side. Loneliness doesn’t disappear because someone is silenced, and exhaustion doesn’t leave because someone has to keep smiling through an unbearable day. Discrimination doesn’t end just because no direct insult was verbalized, and mental distress doesn’t evaporate because it’s ignored.

In 2025, Japanese police handled the bodies of 76,941 people who had lived alone and died at home. That’s 37.6 percent of all bodies handled by police last year. Those figures don’t tell us why each person died. But the reality of solitary death is one of the saddest truths in a city as crowded as Tokyo. Nothing written in this article is an argument against good manners or social order. Those are things that make Tokyo safer and more livable. The danger begins when protecting the image becomes more important than seeing reality.

At the beginning of this story, trash was piled up under a blue net, waiting to be picked up by city workers. By the end, plastic crates and greasy rags were piled up in a hidden alley away from the public. Tokyo’s surface isn’t fake at all. But it’s incomplete. Nothing ceases to exist because it’s hidden from the public. It’s just waiting somewhere else until someone is willing to look.
